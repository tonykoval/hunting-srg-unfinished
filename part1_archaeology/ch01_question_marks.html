The Question Marks

Appendix A of Behbahani's thesis is a graveyard of possibilities: table after table of prime orders and fixed-point counts, almost every row ending in $0$. But a graveyard is exactly where you look for the ones that were never buried.

1.1   The method being recorded

The idea, now standard, was theirs: if a strongly regular graph admits an automorphism $\sigma$ of prime order $p$, then $\sigma$'s orbits partition the vertices into fixed points and $p$-cycles, and the adjacency matrix collapses to a small orbit matrix — a quotient that must satisfy arithmetic constraints inherited from $A^2 = (k-\mu)I + (\lambda-\mu)A + \mu J$. The hunt then has two stages: enumerate all orbit matrices (small, fast), and lift each one back to a full graph by choosing a circulant structure on every orbit block (large, sometimes brutally slow). No orbit matrices means no such automorphism; a lift means a graph.

For every open parameter set under $100$ vertices they published the full run: each prime $p$, each admissible fixed-point count, the number of orbit matrices found, and the number of graphs lifted. The legend defines three endings. A $0$ in the matrix column closes the case outright. A count with 0 graphs found closes it after a finished lift. And then:

The legend's third symbol

"If there is a '?' in the table, it means that we have not found any solutions and we were not able to finish the program due to time constraints." — Appendix A. A question mark is not a result. It is a bookmark left in 2011.

1.2   The three that matter

Most question marks in the appendix sit next to hopeless numbers — the $p=5$ row of $\mathrm{srg}(85,30,11,10)$ reads >30000 orbit matrices with an estimate of $5\times10^4$ days; nobody is finishing that by brute force. But three question marks sit next to small numbers, in the fixed-point-free rows where the theory is cleanest:

$\mathrm{srg}(85,30,11,10)$, $p=17$$5$$2$ with their $f=17$ row already closed (nnfp), a finished lift settles order-$17$ entirely
$\mathrm{srg}(88,27,6,9)$, $p=11$$8$$5$ settles the fixed-point-free order-$11$ case
$\mathrm{srg}(99,42,21,15)$, $p=11$$9$$173$ settles the fixed-point-free order-$11$ case

Each is a handful of matrices over a handful of orbits. What stopped a 2011 workstation — exhausting the circulant choices of a $\mathbb{Z}_{17}$ or $\mathbb{Z}_{11}$ lift — is precisely the kind of bounded search that modern SAT solvers, GPU-batched verification, and a fortnight-hardened pipeline exist for.

1.3   The re-count across fifteen years

Before finishing someone's search, you re-run its first stage. For $\mathrm{srg}(85,30,11,10)$ under a fixed-point-free order-$17$ automorphism, the orbit matrices are $5\times5$, entries $0..17$, even diagonal, rows summing to $30$, quotient identity throughout. Two independent modern enumerations — a CP-SAT model instructed to enumerate all solutions, and this project's Rust generator — agree on exactly two isomorphism classes:

$\begin{pmatrix}10&5&5&5&5\\5&10&5&5&5\\5&5&10&5&5\\5&5&5&10&5\\5&5&5&5&10\end{pmatrix} \qquad \begin{pmatrix}4&5&5&8&8\\5&10&5&5&5\\5&5&10&5&5\\8&5&5&4&8\\8&5&5&8&4\end{pmatrix}$

Traces $50$ and $32$ — both inside the spectral pin $\{14,32,50\}$ forced by eigenvalue integrality, with trace $14$ genuinely empty. Behbahani–Lam's count: $2$. Ours: $2$. Fifteen years, two completely unrelated codebases, the same two matrices. That agreement is mutual: it validates their enumeration and ours in one stroke, and it means the question mark's fate rests entirely on the lift.

Why "?" cases are the right prey

An open parameter set with a small, agreed set of orbit matrices is the rarest thing in this business: a question with a guaranteed ending. Exhaust the lifts and you either hold a new strongly regular graph at an open parameter set, or a clean nonexistence statement for that symmetry — no third outcome, no unbounded residue, no wall. The three books beside this one chase walls. This book collects endings.
